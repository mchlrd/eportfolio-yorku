## Artifact 01 — Isolated malware analysis lab

work sample · cloud security · 2026

Design Use of Engineering Tools Investigation Impact on Society and the Environment

Introduction

Suspicious files needed to be executed and studied without any path back to production. I designed and secured an isolated analysis environment in AWS — EC2 instances inside dedicated VPCs, private subnets, security groups, IAM integrations, and S3 buckets — so that artifacts could detonate and be analysed with confidence that the blast radius was contained. What it demonstrates: designing for containment under real constraints rather than building the happy path and hoping.

reflection

Retell — what I created or did

Analysing potentially harmful artifacts means running them, and doing that anywhere near live infrastructure is negligent. So the first problem was not analysis at all — it was creating somewhere safe enough for analysis to be permitted. I built that environment, then worked through artifacts using PEStudio, Detect It Easy, FakeNet-NG, HxD, and Sysinternals, applying static reverse-engineering techniques to describe malicious behaviour and extract indicators of compromise.

Relate — how this connects to my goals

The engineering design cycle I have studied — define the need, establish constraints, generate and evaluate alternatives, implement — mapped onto this problem more cleanly than any assignment has. The decisive constraint was not technical capability but consequence: the design had to fail safely, because the material was designed to cause harm. That is inseparable from the ethics work in Goal 3, and it is the reason containment mattered at all.

Reflect — what I learned

What I learned about myself is that I default to solving the immediate problem and treating constraints as somebody else's job. My first instinct was to pick the analysis tools; the actual engineering question was the boundary condition. What changed my thinking was realising that the isolation was the deliverable — a brilliant finding produced in an unsafe environment would have been worthless and irresponsible. What I would do differently is write the containment argument down first and let it dictate the tooling, rather than choosing tools and retrofitting a boundary.

Competency

Design — a solution engineered to satisfy competing constraints, analytical capability against strict containment. Use of Engineering Tools — EC2, VPC, IAM, PEStudio, FakeNet-NG and HxD selected and operated to serve a defined objective rather than used for their own sake.

## Artifact 02 — Vulnerability remediation automation

work sample · automation & security operations · 2026

Problem Analysis Use of Engineering Tools Life-Long Learning Professionalism

Introduction

Responding to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue was a manual, repetitive, easily deprioritised process. I developed Python and PowerShell automation using Microsoft Graph, the Azure SDK, REST APIs, and regex parsing to drive KEV remediation, endpoint reporting, and vulnerability validation. What it demonstrates: recognising that the bottleneck in security operations is usually process, not tooling.

reflection

Retell — what I created or did

Every week brought a new list of known-exploited vulnerabilities and a queue of endpoints to check against it. Handled manually the work was slow and, worse, invisible — nobody could see coverage until someone asked. I wrote automation that queried the relevant Microsoft APIs, parsed and matched results, generated reporting, and validated remediation status, with scheduled Function Apps and notification workflows so the pipeline ran and reported without being driven.

Relate — how this connects to my goals

This is the problem-analysis approach the course frames as central to engineering: state the problem before reaching for a solution. The stated problem — "we have vulnerabilities to remediate" — was misleading. The real problem was that remediation depended on a person remembering to do it, which meant the process failed silently whenever workload spiked. Reframing it changed what a solution even looked like. It also sits directly in Goal 2, where the skill is building automation that holds up.

Reflect — what I learned

What I learned about myself is that I am willing to do repetitive work patiently — which sounds virtuous and is actually a trap, because the patience delayed me from questioning whether the work should exist. What changed my thinking was measuring the manual process and realising how much of a security team's week was being absorbed by tasks a script could do better and never forget. Since then I try to ask "should this be automatic?" before "how do I do this faster?". What I would do differently is instrument the automation from the start: a process that cannot report its own coverage is only marginally better than a manual one nobody can see.

Competency

Problem Analysis — reframing a staffing problem as a process problem and then an automation problem. Use of Engineering Tools — Graph, the Azure SDK, REST APIs and regex parsing applied deliberately, each chosen for a specific part of the workflow and documented so it could be maintained by someone else.

## Artifact 03 — Quantifying technical risk

work sample · risk & economics · 2026

Economics and Project Management Problem Analysis Communication Skills Investigation

Introduction

A proposed expansion into SaaS and lead-generation markets hinged on infrastructure that had never been stress-tested against it. Applying the FAIR framework, I quantified financial and operational exposure — Asset Value, Exposure Factors, Single Loss Expectancy, Annualized Loss Expectancy — and used exponential failure models to calculate MTBF, producing a probability and impact picture leadership could actually decide against. What it demonstrates: translating gut-feel risk into numbers somebody can argue with.

reflection

Retell — what I created or did

The team wanted to know whether the existing infrastructure could support a significant expansion without degrading, and "probably" was not a sufficient answer for a decision that size. I modelled the financial exposure of a potential breach using FAIR, calculated reliability metrics including MTBF using exponential failure distributions, and ran threat-modelling SWOT assessments to convert ambiguous market conditions into structured findings. The output was a co-authored, employer-facing strategy report.

Relate — how this connects to my goals

This is the economics competency in a form I had not expected to meet as a security-minded student: the same technical facts, expressed as money and downtime, became a decision instead of an opinion. It also reinforced something the course stresses — that communication is not polish, it is the delivery mechanism for the analysis. An unpresentable risk model helps nobody, which is why goal 3 of my Personal Development Goals targets exactly this weakness.

Reflect — what I learned

What I learned about myself is that I am more comfortable producing an analysis than defending one, and that the discomfort is worth pushing through. Presenting range-based estimates to people who wanted a single number taught me that intellectual honesty often looks like unhelpful hedging until you explain why the range is the answer. What I would do differently is build the probability and impact matrix with the audience in mind from the first day rather than translating it at the end — the framing that finally made it land should have shaped the analysis from the start.

Competency

Economics and Project Management — expressing technical risk in financial and reliability terms that supported a genuine business decision. Problem Analysis — converting an ambiguous question into defined variables with stated assumptions that could be challenged and revised.

## Artifact 04 — Deployment automation

work sample · systems & embedded · 2024

Use of Engineering Tools Individual and Teamwork Knowledge Base for Engineering Design

Introduction

Hardware staging was slow, inconsistent, and hard to hand over. I designed and optimised Linux-based workflows with Shell, C, and Python automation, prototyped ESP32-C3 microcontrollers for automated diagnostics and testing, and built Windows configuration pipelines that ingested Registry values programmatically to enforce baselines consistently. What it demonstrates: my first measured experience of automation changing how a team spends its day.

reflection

Retell — what I created or did

Getting hardware ready involved a manual sequence repeated for every unit — slow, and prone to drifting between whoever ran it. I wrote automation in Shell, C, and Python that reduced deployment time by 20–35% while making the process reproducible, then extended it into embedded work: programming ESP32-C3 boards to run automated diagnostics and tests. On the Windows side I built configuration pipelines that wrote Registry values programmatically, so security baselines were applied identically every time instead of being remembered correctly by a person.

Relate — how this connects to my goals

This is where the knowledge-base competency stopped being coursework and started being a tool: low-level systems knowledge is what made it possible to automate below the operating system, and the scripting was the easy part once the underlying behaviour was understood. It also connects to teamwork in a way I had not anticipated — the value of the automation was not mine, it was the team's, and it only counted once somebody else could run it without me present.

Reflect — what I learned

What I learned about myself is that I am motivated by the moment a process stops being painful for somebody else — that is the feedback loop that keeps me going, more than solving the puzzle did. What changed my thinking was realising that a script only I understood was a single point of failure with good intentions. What I would do differently is write the usage notes first and treat documentation as part of the deliverable rather than a courtesy added afterwards if there is time.

Competency

Use of Engineering Tools — scripting and embedded toolchains applied to a measurable operational problem, a 20–35% reduction in deployment time. Individual and Teamwork — the deliverable was designed to be handed over and run by others rather than kept as personal output.

## Artifact 05 — Handling malicious software, and who carries the risk

ethics & sustainability discussion · 2026

Ethics and Equity Impact on Society and the Environment Professionalism

Introduction

An artifact that is a discussion rather than a build. During my placement I handled material designed to cause harm — executing suspicious files in an isolated environment, and working with highly restricted intelligence alongside federal cyber-security partners. Neither task raised a technical problem I could not solve. Both raised questions I had not been asked to think about: who is exposed when this work goes wrong, who benefits from it, and what the environmental cost of the infrastructure behind it actually is.

reflection

Retell — what I created or did

I built and operated an isolated cloud environment for analysing malicious software, and I took part in incident investigations involving restricted federal intelligence. In both cases the material was sensitive, the surrounding process was strict, and the purpose was protective: understanding an attack so that it could be detected or stopped elsewhere. On the environmental side, analysis environments like the one I built consume real compute — instances, storage, and network capacity that run whether or not anyone is actively using them.

Relate — how this connects to my goals

The ethics material asks who is affected by a technical decision, including people who never agreed to be part of it. In this work the people affected are residents of a county who will never know an incident happened, and in the federal case, members of the public whose safety depends on intelligence being handled correctly. The sustainability question follows the same logic one step further out: every analysis environment that is left running has a cost paid by someone else, in energy and resources. Designing for containment also means designing for disposal, and treating an idle instance as free is exactly the kind of unexamined assumption this goal is meant to expose.

Reflect — what I learned

What changed my thinking was noticing that "it is isolated, so it is fine" is not an ethics position — it is a technical answer to a non-technical question. Isolation protects the systems, but it says nothing about whether the work should be done, who authorised it, or what happens to the people on the other end of the analysis. What I learned about myself is that I default to the engineering frame and find the accountability frame easy to skip, which is precisely the failure mode the competency is warning about. What I would do differently is ask the second question explicitly at the start — not only "can this be contained?" but "who bears the risk if it is not, and have they agreed to it?" I do not think my work was wrong. I do think I reached that conclusion by assumption rather than by reasoning, and that is worth correcting.

Competency

Ethics and Equity — the artifact reasons about who is affected by protective security work, including people with no part in the decision, and about the limits of a purely technical justification. Impact on Society and the Environment — it extends that reasoning to the resource cost of the infrastructure itself, treating an idle analysis environment as an ongoing claim on shared resources rather than a neutral choice.
