C — Goals & Portfolios

Three goals, each with portfolios gathering evidence of progress

Goals are the organising framework for this portfolio. Each goal below holds one or more portfolios, and each portfolio collects documents, artifacts and reflections that show movement rather than arrival. The sequence starts inward — Discover Myself — and then builds outward into the two career goals I am actually working toward.

## Goal 1 — Discover Myself

Before I can argue that I am ready for a career, I need to be honest about what I want, what I am actually good at, and where my judgement is weakest. The four portfolios below are that audit, written down so it can be checked against reality later.

### Portfolio 1.1 — Dreams and Goals

Contents: where I started, why I chose this field, and what I am aiming at. What I hoped to learn: whether my stated goals are genuinely mine, or just the path of least resistance.

▸ artifact: why computer science — and why security Life-Long Learning

reflection

Retell — what I created or did

I wrote a short account of how I got here: the early curiosity, the decision to study Computer Science at Lassonde, and the point at which security stopped being a subject I was passing and became the thing I cared about.

Relate — how this connects to my goals

This is the "why" underneath every other entry in the portfolio. Professional development is something you build deliberately rather than something that happens to you, and this artifact is the claim that my interest is genuine rather than a response to what seemed employable. It also sets the standard the other portfolios are measured against.

Reflect — what I learned

Writing it down forced me to separate two things I had been conflating: wanting to be good at something, and wanting to do it. What changed my thinking was noticing that my strongest memories are all of problems I could not solve at first. I now want to choose based on where the hard problems are, not where the comfortable ones are — and to re-read this each year to check whether that is actually what I am doing.

Competency

Life-Long Learning — it documents a pattern of deliberately pursuing material beyond what was required, and establishes the self-directed standard the rest of the portfolio is measured against.

Personal Development Goals (SMART)

Goals period: October 2026 – September 2027. Each target has a date, a measure, and the evidence I will keep.

1 — Communication skills · December 2026 and April 2027

By 4 December 2026 I will give a 10-minute briefing in plain language to the non-technical County staff on how the automated threat-intelligence pipeline protects the organisation, and prepare a two-page handover guide for the IT team. The measure is written feedback from my supervisor and at least three attendees — clarity, structure, pacing, jargon and usefulness — with a target average of 4 out of 5, and no restricted detail in either document. By 15 April 2027 I will give two further talks of 8–10 minutes on security topics and publish one 800-word article for a non-technical audience, raising my self-rating on the same five criteria from 5/10 to 8/10.

2 — Collaboration and leadership · February and April 2027

By 28 February 2027 I will join a cybersecurity or computer-science team at York and take a defined leadership role on one project or competition: at least eight weekly meetings with written agendas, the work tracked in GitHub Projects, and one retrospective at the end. Anonymous team feedback scored on organisation, inclusiveness and communication is the measure, with a target average of 4 out of 5. From 15 January to 30 April 2027 I will also mentor two first- or second-year students through a York programme, in at least eight one-hour sessions each, with one learning objective per mentee and a written record of progress.

3 — Curiosity and imagination · January and June 2027

By 31 January 2027 I will finish the Stanford Cryptography course with at least 80% on every graded assignment, and by 15 February 2027 write a one-page reflection on how two topics from it — key exchange and authenticators — apply to systems I have worked on, such as OAuth2 and Entra ID authentication. By 30 June 2027 I will design and publish an original security tool for a problem I met on placement, with at least three iterations documented in a public GitHub repository, including the attempts that failed, and feedback from at least one mentor or professor.

reflection

Retell — what I created or did

I cross-checked what I claim about myself against what I have actually produced — code, reports, working systems. Where the two agreed I kept the claim; where the claim ran ahead of the evidence I moved it to a development area. The remaining gaps became three SMART goals rather than three intentions.

Relate — how this connects to my goals

Goal-setting is a skill with a structure, and SMART is that structure. A vague intention like "get better at presenting" cannot be checked by anyone; "average 4 out of 5 on a five-question feedback form by 4 December" can. This is what makes the portfolio an evidence record rather than a list of intentions, which is exactly what Goal 1 is meant to establish.

Reflect — what I learned

The uncomfortable part was goal 1. My assessments were strongest where I already had proof and weakest at the thing that decides whether good work is ever adopted: explaining it to someone who does not share my vocabulary. I had been treating communication as a soft skill to pick up later. Writing it as a measurable goal with feedback attached is what turned it into something I will actually be held to. What I would do differently is start collecting that feedback now rather than after graduation.

Competency

Life-Long Learning — converting self-assessment into measurable, dated goals with defined evidence. Professionalism — naming a genuine weakness and committing to a process for correcting it.

### Portfolio 1.3 — Values and Personality

Contents: values-clarification exercises, emotional intelligence (EQ) results with reflection, and how my learning style shows up in a workplace. What I hoped to learn: how I behave when a team is under pressure and the answer is not obvious.

Values (clarified)
Accuracy over speed. Owning mistakes without being asked. Not pretending to understand something I do not. Protecting the people who are not in the room when a decision is made.
Empathy
89 out of 110 — high in both affective empathy (sensing what others feel) and cognitive empathy (taking their perspective).
Reading expressions
10 out of 20 — below average at reading faces, and the one result here that contradicts how I see myself.
Personality profile
Cautious first, then Supportive and Dominant — methodical, quality-driven, reserved until I am certain, and slow to speak before I have the answer.
Learning style, observed
Iterative and verification-driven: I understand a system by changing one thing, watching what breaks, and writing down why.
reflection

Retell — what I created or did

In my third week on placement a data-format mismatch between Cisco Firepower and Microsoft Defender over hash types stopped the pipeline. My profile describes me as self-reliant, and that week it cost me: instead of asking the two colleagues who had configured those platforms, I spent hours in vendor documentation and went to them only after another half-day had gone. The fix took them minutes.

Relate — how this connects to my goals

Two scores in this portfolio point at the same thing from opposite directions. Empathy at 89/110 says that I register what other people are feeling; reading expressions at 10/20 says that I miss what is in front of me unless it is said out loud. Together the gap is not caring too little — it is assuming I already know, and therefore not asking. Collaboration and contextual awareness appear as development areas on my co-op self-evaluation for the same reason, and every role I am aiming at involves work other people have to plan around.

Reflect — what I learned

What I learned is that self-reliance is a strength with a cost: it protects the quality of my output and quietly degrades everyone else's ability to plan around me. What changed my thinking was the half-day I spent proving I did not need help, against the minutes it took two colleagues to answer. The rule I work to now is thirty minutes: if I am still in documentation with no hypothesis, I ask, and I say what I have already ruled out so the question is cheap to answer. A status update is not overhead — it is how a team allocates trust.

Competency

Individual and Teamwork — it analyses a concrete collaboration failure and names the behaviour change that addresses it, rather than describing teamwork in the abstract.

## Goal 3 — Protect public & critical infrastructure

The longest-term goal, and the one that gives the others a reason. It is about applying technical depth where the consequences are real — services people depend on without ever thinking about them.

### Portfolio 3.1 — Public-sector practice

Contents: what working inside a public institution actually taught me. What I hoped to learn: whether the constraints of public-sector engineering are limitations or the point.

My current placement is my first sustained experience of infrastructure that carries public consequence: identity systems that gate access to services, and vulnerability work where a missed remediation is not a metric but a risk to residents. The most useful thing I have learned is that the hard part is rarely the technology — it is the accountability, the documentation, and the fact that the people affected are not in the room.

### Portfolio 3.2 — Ethics, sustainability and responsibility

Contents: the ethical and sustainability questions my work raises, and how I have reasoned about them. What I hoped to learn: how to hold a technical decision and its consequences in the same thought.

  • ▸Handling malicious software, and who carries the risk Ethics and Equity Impact on Society and the Environment

The artifacts around this one answer what I built. This one answers what the work was for, and who carried the risk — which is the question that decides whether the rest of it should have been done at all.
